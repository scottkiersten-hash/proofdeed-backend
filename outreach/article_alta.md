# Deed Fraud Is Happening After Closing — Here's the Technology That Stops It

**By Scott Kiersten, Founder & CEO, ProofDeed LLC**

---

The FBI's Boston Division issued a warning in early 2025 that quit claim deed fraud was rising sharply. New York's Attorney General reported a 240% increase in deed theft complaints. An 85-year-old Atlanta homeowner had her property stolen through a forged deed that looked entirely legitimate on paper.

These cases share a common thread: the fraud happened after the document was recorded. And every title company involved faced the same impossible question — how do you prove what a document said at the moment of closing, months or years later?

The answer, increasingly, is blockchain document certification.

---

## The Problem With Reactive Protection

Current deed fraud protection is largely reactive. Property alert services notify homeowners after a document is filed. Fraud investigations begin after the damage is done. Title insurance pays out after litigation concludes.

What's missing is a cryptographic record of what every document contained at the exact moment of closing — before any alteration is possible.

This is not a theoretical gap. It is the gap that fraudsters exploit every time they record a forged deed, alter a closing document, or manufacture a backdated agreement. The document looks legitimate because it was processed through legitimate channels. The problem is that nothing proves what the original said.

---

## How Blockchain Certification Works

Blockchain document certification does not store your documents. It does not replace your closing software, your title plant, or your existing workflows. It adds a single cryptographic step at the moment of recording.

When a document is submitted for certification, the system computes a SHA-256 hash — a unique 64-character mathematical fingerprint of that document's exact contents. If even one character in the document changes after certification, the fingerprint no longer matches. Fraud becomes mathematically detectable.

That fingerprint is anchored to the Polygon public blockchain in a single transaction, creating an immutable, timestamped record that any party — a court, an auditor, opposing counsel, or a title examiner — can verify independently without contacting your office.

The resulting certificate satisfies Federal Rule of Evidence Rule 901(b)(9), which establishes authentication standards for evidence produced by a process that produces an accurate result. Courts in multiple jurisdictions have recognized blockchain-anchored hash evidence as meeting this standard.

## What This Means for Title Companies

For title companies, blockchain certification addresses three specific liability exposures:

**Post-closing document alteration.** A buyer claims the terms of the purchase agreement were different from what was signed. A seller disputes what was disclosed. A lender challenges the closing disclosure. Without a certified record of what each document contained at closing, the dispute becomes your word against theirs. A blockchain certificate ends that dispute immediately.

**Chain of custody in deed fraud cases.** When a fraudulent deed is recorded, investigators need to establish what documents existed, when, and in what form. A blockchain-certified closing package creates an unbroken chain of custody from the moment of execution — making it impossible to introduce altered documents into the record without detection.

**Regulatory and compliance documentation.** Title companies operate under significant regulatory scrutiny. Blockchain-certified compliance records — including ALTA Best Practices documentation, wire instructions, and closing instructions — provide an independently verifiable audit trail that satisfies examiner requirements without requiring access to your internal systems.

## Implementation: What It Actually Looks Like

The most common question I hear from title operations managers is: "What does this require us to change?"

The answer is almost nothing. Document certification via API is a single call per document — integrated into existing document management workflows in days, not months. For companies that prefer a manual workflow, documents can be uploaded through a web portal at the moment of closing. Either way, the certificate is generated and delivered within seconds.

Volume pricing makes this cost-effective at scale. A title company processing 500 closings per month can certify every closing document — purchase agreements, closing disclosures, title commitments, deeds, and affidavits — for a fraction of the cost of a single fraud-related dispute.

## The Broader Picture

Deed fraud is not slowing down. The combination of remote notarization, digital document workflows, and increasingly sophisticated forgery tools has made post-closing document fraud more accessible than at any point in the industry's history.

The title industry built its foundation on the principle that land records should be trustworthy, permanent, and verifiable. Blockchain document certification extends that principle into the digital age — creating a record that is not just trustworthy, but mathematically provable.

Title companies that adopt this technology now will be positioned to demonstrate a standard of care that their peers cannot match — in court, in regulatory examinations, and in the market.
